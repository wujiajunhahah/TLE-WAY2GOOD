01The problem we work on

Seeing, understanding and responding
are different needs.

This process recurs in the accounts. It is not a fixed experience shared by every owner.

  1. 01 Time apart Work, overseas study or distance
  2. 02 Think of your pet Missing them or worrying about a risk
  3. 03 Open the camera See where they are and what they do
  4. 04 Questions remain Resting—or not feeling well?
  5. 05 Hard to respond No time, too far away or no local help

The gap we are exploring

A picture gives information, but not always reassurance.
You may need everyday moments, gentle contact or someone who can be there.

02Interview findings

The same camera.
Different reasons to care.

Five interviews revealed a desire for connection, a need for timely care, and a case where the existing arrangement already feels sufficient.

What already works

  • Family or workers cover daily feeding
  • Cameras provide basic safety checks
  • Brief calls, photos and videos offer contact
  • Familiar environments and established routines

What can remain difficult

  • Quiet scenes leave room for speculation
  • Time zones make active moments easy to miss
  • Long recordings take effort to review
  • Seeing a problem does not make local help available

03Who we design for

Not every pet owner.

We are starting with people closely bonded to their cats who cannot always be there, while distinguishing what each person needs.

  • People with regular periods awayLess repetitive checking; more meaningful everyday moments
  • People separated by study and time zonesConnection that works without being online together
  • People caring from a distanceClear responsibilities, confirmed handovers and available local help

Cats are an initial focus: 20 of 31 respondents selected “cat”, and one selected “both cat and dog”. This exploratory sample does not represent the whole market.

From “sounds useful”

As of 6 October 2026, 31 valid responses offer initial signals. Each figure below uses 31 as its denominator and describes self-reports in this sample.

  • 16 / 31

    Selected disturbing or frightening the pet as a concern (51.6%; up to two choices).

  • 22 / 31

These are reported experiences and preferences, not purchase commitments. Interviews and field notes also remind us that more interaction is not always better.

05Design opportunity

From repeated checking
to more useful connection.

TodayRemote Monitoring

  • Check a live viewSee location and activity; avoid equating quietness with loneliness
  • Speak through a deviceSome owners feel close; others worry about the pet searching for them
  • Review recordingsTime zones and replay effort make moments easy to miss
  • Contact a local caregiverKnow who is available and who takes responsibility

The opportunityRemote Companionship

Directions to test: everyday clips, optional interaction and confirmed practical care.

  • Collect everyday momentsReduce replay effort and preserve real moments from the day
  • Notice voluntary approachesRecord visible behaviour without claiming to read emotions
  • Keep the owner in controlSet time, duration and action limits; let the pet disengage
  • Confirm care and handoverConnect a concern with a person who can act

06Concept sketches

Make connection useful.
Let the pet choose.

Interaction directions for future testing, not implemented features or pet-emotion assessments.

  • BEHAVIOURConcept · visible action

    Your pet approaches the device

    An approach is a recorded action, not automatic evidence of loneliness or a request for intervention.

  • MOMENTSConcept · asynchronous contact

    A short moment worth watching

    Keep up with everyday life without watching continuously or searching long recordings.

  • BOUNDARIESConcept · easy to stop

    Rest without unsolicited interaction

    The owner sets the limits. The pet can choose not to participate.

07Design principles

The question we’re answering

When we cannot be there,
how can we feel informed and connected
while respecting our pet’s natural rhythm?

  • InformedClear observationsDescribe visible behaviour without turning speculation into an emotional diagnosis.
  • ConnectedReal momentsShare everyday life without requiring the owner to remain online.
  • RespectfulChoice and restKeep interaction gentle and optional; a lack of response is not a failure.
  • SupportivePractical carePeople and responsibilities matter. A device supports the arrangement.

08Next steps

Four things to test next.

  1. 01Do clips really save effort?Compare a simple daily-clips prototype with existing replay habits and observe actual use.
  2. 02Which signals deserve a notification?Learn when information is useful and when it adds worry or interruption.
  3. 03Does the pet choose to participate?Observe approach, indifference and disengagement; test bounded, optional interaction.
  4. 04Who can provide practical care?Test feeding confirmation, holiday handovers and responsibilities in distance-care settings.

Where we are

We have five interviews, 31 survey responses, 15 media sources and barbershop field notes. Next, prototypes will test actual use, pet responses and care handover. There is no product available to buy yet.

A few things you might wonder

How is this different from a pet camera?

We are testing three needs: easier access to real everyday moments, optional interaction within owner-defined limits, and care handover when distance prevents a response. These directions come from interviews and the survey. They remain concepts, with no claim to identify pet emotions.

Can I buy or use something yet?

No. We’re at the research and concept stage, and there is no finished product. If prototype testing opens up, we’ll let subscribers know by email.

What will I receive if I subscribe?
