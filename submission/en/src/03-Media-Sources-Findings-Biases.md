# Media Secondary Source Research: Sources, Key Findings and Biases

**Project**: Remote Pet Companionship (Group 6)
**HMW**: How might we help pet owners feel informed and connected with their cats during everyday separation, while respecting the cat's natural behaviour and rest?
**Research method**: Media (secondary source research) — public sources obtained through web searches, all links actually accessed on **2026-10-06**
**Search tools**: web_search + web_fetch (actual retrieval, not memory-generated)
**Number of sources**: 15 (of which 12 were successfully retrieved in full text and 3 were partially retrieved, noted in the relevant entries)

> **Discipline statement**: This document records only what was actually retrieved. Any content whose full text could not be opened and which could only be seen through a search snapshot is explicitly flagged in the relevant entry. Every figure is attached to a specific source number. All statements by industry and by others are written as "a source states that…" and are not treated as this project's conclusions (this project's conclusions must be supported by our own Interview / Survey / Observation).
> **Note on comparability**: The cat-related behavioural evidence in this document comes largely from small-sample studies in Europe and North America and cannot be directly equated with our 31 questionnaire responses (93.5% mainland China, 64.5% cat owners, 93.5% office workers or students). See Section 3 for details.

**S4 | PLOS ONE: a controlled experiment on cats left alone for 30 minutes vs 4 hours (Eriksson et al., 2017)**
- Experimental design: **14** cats from private, **single-cat households**, each receiving two treatments — being left at home alone for **30 minutes (T0.5)** and for **4 hours (T4)**; the behaviour of the cat and the owner was recorded before, during and after the separation.
- Result one (during separation): the proportion of time cats spent "lying down and resting" during separation was 0.27±0.1 for T0.5 and 0.58±0.08 for T4 (P = 0.02) — **the longer the cats were alone, the more they rested**; the two treatments did not differ in the last two 5-minute intervals of the post-separation period, "indicating that the cats were not affected by the duration of separation". The authors' original conclusion: "it seemed as cats coped well with being left alone".
- Result two (at reunion): **after a longer separation, the cats purred (purr, P = 0.03) and stretched (stretch, P = 0.04) significantly more**, and this was not correlated with the owner's verbal interaction behaviour. On this basis the authors propose a "rebound of contact-seeking behaviour", **implying that the owner is an important part of the cat's social environment**.
- Background cited: the paper notes that cats normally rest **16–18 hours** a day; separation-related problems in cats (inappropriate elimination, excessive vocalisation, destruction, over-grooming) occur in a minority of cats only when the owner is absent (citing Schwartz 2002, a longitudinal study). The paper also concedes that "most cats do not show abnormal behaviour when separated from their owners, but **no study** has investigated how these cats respond to being left alone at home" (that is, this is a gap in the field).
- Use: this source supports two opposing arguments at once ("cats can be left alone" and "cats care about their owner"), which is precisely the evidential basis for the tension in our HMW around "respecting the cat's natural behaviour and rest".

**S5 | MSD Veterinary Manual: social behaviour of cats (full text reviewed July 2025)**
- Social structure: cats' social structure is **flexible**; under feral conditions they live in small groups centred on the queen and her offspring; "cats spend most of their time away from other individuals", and human–cat interaction shows similar characteristics.
- The key defining sentence: "**Cats are social animals but solitary hunters**".
- Routine: cats spend about 40% of their time sleeping, 22% resting and 15% grooming (citing Panaman 1981), and are **crepuscular** animals. Cats prefer to eat about 13 small meals a day.
- Senses: compared with humans, cats identify objects better in low light and can **hear sounds in the ultrasonic range**.
- **The five pillars of a healthy feline environment**: ① provide a safe place; ② provide **multiple, separated** core environmental resources (food, water, elimination area, scratching area, play area, resting area); ③ provide opportunities for play and predatory behaviour; ④ provide positive, consistent and predictable human–cat social interaction; ⑤ provide an environment that respects the importance of the cat's sense of smell.
- Resource competition: "**Many cats prefer not to share core resources**"; in multi-cat or multi-pet households, cats adopt a "**time sharing**" strategy to reduce direct conflict; providing several copies of the same resource in scattered locations allows each cat to obtain resources with minimal competition; **even in single-cat households**, providing multiple resource points allows the cat to "reduce encounters with potential environmental stressors (other pets/dogs, children, guests, noise) by navigating its environment".
- Sources of stress: "**A predictable and consistent routine is important. An inconsistent routine can be a source of chronic stress for cats**", which in turn leads to more illness behaviours and elimination outside the tray. Strong odours (cleaning agents, citrus, scented litter) cause unnecessary stress.
- Principles of interaction: positive and **predictable** human–cat interaction greatly benefits cats, but "depends on the cat's own perception of what is positive, not on what the human family considers positive"; **positive punishment (shouting, hitting, spraying water) damages the human–cat relationship and may provoke aggression**, and should be avoided.
- Use: the five pillars + "not sharing core resources" + "consistency of routine" directly constitute the design red lines for this project's "controlled automatic interaction".

**S6 | AAFP/FelineVMA 2024 guidelines on intercat tension (news release 2024-07-17)**
- A source states that: intercat tension is "**very common but often overlooked because most of the signals are subtle**"; it **can also occur in two-cat households**, and "**most often occurs when a new cat is introduced into the home**" (citing the guidelines' co-chair Dr Ilona Rodan).
- The guidelines were developed by a dedicated panel of veterinarians and animal behaviourists, adapting the "five pillars" into a framework for preventing and managing intercat tension in multi-cat households; the content includes "**debunking 10 common myths**", visual examples of intercat tension, a case appendix and a systematic treatment algorithm.
- Use: this shows that "resource competition and hidden tension in multi-cat households" is a real problem backed by official clinical guidelines, and that such tension **is almost impossible to spot with a single camera looking down at the living room**.

**S7 | Cats' responses to the owner's voice and to "cat-directed speech" (de Mouzon et al., Animal Cognition 2022)**
- Sample: **16** indoor domestic cats; pre-recorded audio of the owner and of a stranger was played, with the phrases spoken in "**cat-directed speech**" and in "adult-directed speech" respectively.
- Result one: on hearing a stranger call their name three times in a row, **10/16 cats showed a decrease in behavioural intensity**; when the **owner's voice** was then played, behavioural intensity rose significantly again, expressed as **turning the ears towards the speaker, increased movement around the room and pupil dilation**. The authors consider this to "indicate that cats can distinguish the owner's voice from a stranger's".
- Result two: when the owner used **cat-directed speech**, the behavioural intensity of 10 cats rose significantly; whereas **for the stranger, the cats showed no different response regardless of the register used**.
- The authors' conclusion: cat–human communication "may rely on experience with the speaker's voice", and "**a one-to-one relationship is important for cats to form strong bonds with humans**".
- Limitations (self-reported in the paper): the sample size is small and may not represent the behaviour of all cats; replication is recommended in future with "more socialised cats that are more used to interacting with strangers".
- Use: this is the most direct piece of evidence for this project's "audio interaction" design.

## 2. Key Findings

### Key Finding 1 | The market is large, but the cell occupied by "smart companionship hardware" is small; consumers already admit that they are "buying companionship"
- **Conclusion**: a source states that in 2025 China's urban dog and cat consumer market was 312.6 billion yuan with 72.89 million pet cats (S1); the pet economy industry was 811.4 billion yuan (S2). But in the consumption structure **supplies account for only 12.2% and services only 6.5%, with food + healthcare together making up 81.3%** (S1). At the same time 44.50% of consumers list "stress relief" and 42.16% list "emotional companionship" as their main reason for keeping a pet (S2), and the weight of "checking on the pet's condition in real time" in boarding decisions has risen sharply compared with 2024 (S1).
- **Supporting sources**: S1, S2 (background data S3, S11)
- **Implications for the project**: the emotional need is **admitted by consumers themselves**, but spending is still concentrated on "eating" and "going to the vet". We cannot assume that willingness to pay exists just because the need is real — the 31 questionnaires and 5 interviews must answer "**who pays, how much, once or monthly**". S1's "rising weight of checking on the pet's condition in real time" is our most favourable demand-side signal, because it appears in a **boarding scenario where payment behaviour has already occurred**.

### Key Finding 2 | "Living alone + young pet owners" is a structural trend, and our target group holds up at the macro level (but only as far as "the group holds up")
- **Conclusion**: a source states that in 2024 one-person households accounted for about 19.5%, and that by 2030 China's population living alone may reach 150–200 million with a solo-living rate possibly exceeding 30% (S3); among pet owners, those born in the 1990s account for 42.7% and those born in the 2000s 26.3% (S1); *Ban Yue Tan* and the experts interviewed explicitly list "building **stable and low-burden emotional connections** through **keeping pets**" as one of the ways to respond to the emotional anxiety of young people living alone (S3); the companionship economy is defined as a rigid demand generated by "solo living, population ageing and a fast-paced life" (S2).
- **Supporting sources**: S3, S1, S2
- **Implications for the project**: our user profile ("has a strong emotional bond with a pet but cannot be present for long periods") is aligned with the direction of the social trend, and can be backed in the deck with macro data. But **macro statistics cannot be extrapolated to individual behaviour**: a high share of one-person households ≠ young people living alone will buy remote companionship devices. That leap must be made by our own Interview/Survey; Media is responsible only for showing that "this group exists and is growing".

### Key Finding 3 | Cats do not need "constant companionship", but they care about "who is with them and how" — contact-seeking rebounds after separation, and they respond only to the owner's voice and cat-directed register
- **Conclusion**: a source states that, compared with 30 minutes, when cats were left at home alone for 4 hours **there was no difference in behaviour during the separation and they in fact rested more**, and the authors conclude that the cats "coped well with being left alone"; but **after the owner returned, cats that had been separated longer purred and stretched significantly more**, interpreted as a "rebound of contact-seeking behaviour", indicating that the owner is an important part of the cat's social environment (S4). At the same time, cats can **distinguish the owner's voice from a stranger's**, and behavioural intensity rose significantly only when **the owner used "cat-directed speech"** — changing the register made no difference for a stranger (S7). Authoritative veterinary material defines cats as "social animals + solitary hunters" that "spend most of their time away from other individuals", and stresses "positive, consistent and **predictable** human–cat interaction" (S5).
- **Supporting sources**: S4, S7, S5
- **Implications for the project**: three design constraints follow directly — ① remote voice must use **a sample of the owner's own voice** and a **cat-directed register**, not a generic alert tone, a machine-synthesised voice or a stranger's voice (otherwise, by the mechanism in S7, the cat is very likely not to respond); ② interaction should be **low-frequency, predictable and something the cat can opt out of**, rather than "the owner connecting whenever they feel like it"; ③ "it didn't respond when I called" is, for a cat, a **normal behavioural outcome**, and the product should not present "no response" to the owner as a failure or a fault, since that would create anxiety rather than reassurance (echoing S11's "reassurance turns into crime-solving anxiety").

### Key Finding 4 | The real pain points of existing pet cameras are not "not seeing clearly enough" but **false alerts, startling, dropouts and subscriptions**; multi-cat households fail further
- **Conclusion**: genuine user complaints are highly concentrated in these categories: **false alerts** ("the bark alert goes off if one of my dogs so much as rustles the pee pads"; noise mistaken for barking and a non-human mistaken for a human), **two-way audio so poor that it scares the pet** ("when you try to talk to it, it scares them"; "it sounds like a potato"), **dropouts/slowness** ("it has worked properly maybe only 40% of the time"), and **non-transparent subscription charges** ("after 2 years smart alerts became a $6.99/month subscription") (S8); on the Chinese side there are likewise complaints about AI identifying **a single black-and-white cat** as a "yellow dog / orange animal / ginger cat", and black-humour anxiety about late-night false alerts of the "**buy a monitoring camera, get a puzzle free**" kind (S11). One review snapshot also contains a cat owner stating explicitly that the voice feature "**scares my cats rather than comfort them**" (S15). More importantly: the question multi-cat households care about most in the community is "**can it tell multiple cats apart**" (S11), while veterinary guidelines state that intercat tension is "very common but often overlooked because most of the signals are subtle" (S6).
- **Supporting sources**: S8, S11, S15, S6 (supplemented by the stress principles in S5)
- **Implications for the project**: the differentiation opportunity **lies not in "higher resolution, wider angle" but in "less disturbance + more reliable judgement"**. Specifically: ① by default **do not make sounds or move suddenly**, treating "risk of startling" as a first-order design metric; ② alerts must include **false-alert suppression and explainability** (better to under-report than to send a false alert at night); ③ for multi-cat households, individual identification is a question of **whether the product is usable at all**, not a bonus feature (the 139 comments in S11 already show this is the first question); ④ the value of alerts should sit on **the three signal points of food bowl / water bowl / litter tray** (the first-hand experience on camera placement in S11), not on "seeing what the cat is up to".

### Key Finding 5 | Competitors have turned "companionship" into "hardware + subscription", but their features are heavily dog-oriented, and their cat lines go only as far as "meow alerts"
- **Conclusion**: the Furbo Nanny subscription is **$6.99–$8.74/month** (multi-camera +$2/month each, cloud recording 3 days/7 days) (S12); the Petcube Bites 2 Lite is **£149.95** with an official rating of 4.3/5 (1,512 ratings) (S13); the Enabot Ebo Air 2 is **€230** and was judged by the reviewer to be "**far from essential**" and to leave "**the cat less impressed**" (S14); the Furbo hardware on Amazon is split into two SKUs, "no subscription (no AI alerts) CA$199" and "subscription required (3 months minimum)" (S15). Furbo already has a dedicated Cat Camera line, but its cat-specific capabilities remain concentrated in Meowing Alerts (S12); Petcube's claim is "natural, lifelike sound so your pet recognises your voice" (S13) — and the research in S7 shows this claim is partly valid (cats do distinguish the owner's voice), while user feedback in S8/S15 shows that **in practice the voice pipeline often fails to achieve this effect**.
- **Supporting sources**: S12, S13, S14, S15 (supplemented by the subscription complaints in S8)
- **Implications for the project**: ① **the industry's default answer for the business model is subscription, and subscription happens to be the most concentrated source of negative reviews** (S8), so we must design carefully what "free" should include; ② competitors' capabilities in the cat scenario stop at "hear a meow → notify you", and **do not address "understanding + controlled interaction that does not disturb"**, which is our opening; ③ the media verdict that the €230 Enabot is "far from essential" is a warning about the price ceiling.

## 3. Potential Biases and Limitations

### B1 | Vendor ecosystems and commercial content pollution: the ranking of search results itself favours "advertorials with affiliate links and promotional hooks"
- **Phenomenon**: in searches for this direction, the top-ranking "in-depth review" was a Sohu account article that **carries its own statement that "this article contains AI-generated content"** (S10): its conclusions are one-sidedly favourable to the Furbo Mini throughout, it cites **third parties I cannot verify** such as the "third-party laboratory LabPet 2026.04 report", "iFlytek AI Lab 2026.03 measurements" and "iResearch *2026 Smart Pet Hardware White Paper*", and it closes by tying in a "price protection for 618" promotion. Another widely cited left-behind guide on the Chinese side (S11), although it cites a great deal of first-hand comment, is itself **AIGC aggregation content**, and it points out that the "dedicated litter tray camera" post it cites **is itself promotional**; it also offers a method of identification — "if the body text talks about the trouble in your home and the comments ask about price and what went wrong, it is probably first-hand; if it is selling points throughout plus 'just buy it', treat it as advertising". S5 (vendor website copy) and S14 (a media review, potentially influenced by loan units/public relations) likewise carry a standpoint.
- **Impact**: our initial understanding of "what user pain points look like" may already have been shaped by **the problem framing that vendors want to establish in users' minds** (for example, vendors want you to care about "an AI recognition accuracy with a 2.1% false alert rate", rather than "I should not be woken up in the middle of the night at all").
- **Response**: wherever this document discusses competitor capabilities, it uses only **features and prices verifiable on official websites** (S12/S13) and **traceable third-party assessments** (S14), and explicitly discards figures that cannot be verified (the 72%, 2.1%, 382ms and 18.6 minutes per yuan in S10 are **all excluded**).

### B2 | Self-selection and survivorship bias in review channels: the same product scores 4.8 in the official store but is 67.1% negative on an aggregation site
- **Phenomenon**: the Furbo App scores **4.8/5** on the Apple App Store (page metadata in S8) / **4.7/5 (276 ratings)** in the Taiwan region (S9); whereas JustUseApp's NLP analysis of **5,461** reviews yields "**67.1% negative experiences**" (S8). Neither figure can represent the true distribution of satisfaction: ratings in the official store are affected by **pop-ups prompting reviews** (S8 itself contains a user complaining that "the app is stuck in a loop forcing you to rate it and you can't close it"), while an aggregation site gathers **people who have come specifically to complain**. In S8 one user states the mechanism outright: "**All the reviews that are positive have just received the product and haven't lost the 'premium features' yet**" — that is, **survivorship bias along the time dimension**: satisfaction falls systematically once subscription charges begin.
- **Impact**: neither side's ratings or negative proportion can be used as evidence of "user satisfaction", nor to estimate market size or the strength of demand.
- **Response**: Key Finding 4 in Section 2 uses the reviews only to **identify the types of pain point and the language used** (false alerts, startling, dropouts, subscriptions), not to **infer proportions or prevalence**; prevalence can be answered only by our own 31 questionnaires.

### B3 | Sample coverage and non-comparable measurement bases: small European and North American experimental samples + community posts from first-tier cities do not equal cat owners in mainland China
- **Phenomenon**: ① the samples of the cat behaviour evidence are very small and highly specific — S4 is **14 cats from single-cat households in Sweden** and S7 is **16 domestic cats in France**, and the authors of both studies explicitly flag the sample limitations; S4 even concedes that "**no study**" has investigated how cats that show no abnormal behaviour respond to being left alone. ② S5/S6 are **veterinary clinical guidelines**, whose knowledge derives disproportionately from clinical cases, so there may be "clinical bias" (cats with problems are more likely to be observed). ③ The Chinese first-hand material (Xiaohongshu/Zhihu/SMZDM citations) is dominated by **young, urban, female, internet-active** people, and lacks lower-tier markets, older pet owners and people who do not use social platforms (S11). ④ The **measurement bases of the macro figures differ from one another**: the "urban dog and cat consumer market of 312.6 billion" (S1) and the "pet economy industry of 811.4 billion" (S2) are not the same thing, and mixing them would double-count and exaggerate. ⑤ S3's "population living alone" is a statistic at the level of **population/households**, a different set from "young people living alone who keep cats".
- **Impact**: transferring "cats can adapt to being alone for 4 hours" directly to "our users work 9 hours a day" is a risky inference; treating "complaints from Xiaohongshu users in first-tier cities" as complaints from users nationwide is equally risky.
- **Response**: every behavioural conclusion in this document is labelled with its source and sample size; any inference that enters the deck must state its **sample boundary** ("Among our surveyed respondents…"); subsequent design validation should prioritise **our own Observation** (the cat's real movement paths at home, camera placement and blind spots) to test whether these secondary conclusions hold for our users.

**One further limitation worth remembering separately**: the reviews in S8 come almost entirely from **dog owners** (Furbo is a dog camera brand), and although the Chinese material in S11 is mainly about cats, I **cannot access its original posts** (Xiaohongshu/Zhihu) directly (Zhihu returns 403, Xiaohongshu requires login). So for "cat owners' complaints about pet cameras" we effectively have only **second-hand citation evidence**, and no first-hand platform evidence.

## 4. Media Literacy Self-Assessment

> Addressing the course's four questions: **Who made this content? What is the purpose behind it? What biases exist? How would different people understand it differently?** — answered source category by source category.

### A. Industry white papers and official media reprints (S1)
- **Who made it**: the white paper was produced by the Paidu Pet Industry Big Data Platform, under the guidance of the National Technical Committee for Companion Animal (Pet) Standardisation, the Pet Industry Branch of the China Animal Agriculture Association and the **China Veterinary Medical Association**; what this document read is **CCTV.com's reprinted report**, not the original white paper.
- **Purpose**: an annual industry review and a show of strength, serving pet industry practitioners and investors — it needs to demonstrate that "this industry is growing".
- **Biases**: ① **industry optimism bias**: the choice of measurement basis favours "growth" (emphasising +4.1%, +5.2%); ② **loss in reprinting**: CCTV.com excerpted only the "key data" selected by the publisher, and the provenance of the figures and the statistical method (sample size, cities sampled, questionnaire or estimation) **are not disclosed**, so we cannot judge the denominator; ③ **survivor measurement basis**: the white paper counts "urban dogs and cats", excluding rural and free-roaming cats.
- **How different people understand it differently**: a brand reading "312.6 billion yuan, with cats growing faster than dogs" sees **an opportunity to enter**; a veterinarian reading "feline lower urinary tract disease accounts for 10.2% of reasons for visits" sees **health risk**; while our project reading "supplies + services together only 18.7%" should read it as **a warning** — a need being perceived does not mean the category has been validated.

### B. Industry articles from research institutions (S2)
- **Who made it**: iiMedia Research, a third-party research institution whose business model is selling reports; what this document read is its **free content page**.
- **Purpose**: dual — both to publish industry insight and to **funnel traffic to the institution's paid reports** (there are multiple "get the report" entry points on the page, and the article itself promotes the *Smart Pet Keeping Becomes a Boom — 2026 Research Report on Consumption Trends in the Pet Economy Industry*).
- **Biases**: ① **conclusions serve a sellable narrative**: judgements such as "trillion-yuan companionship economy" and "rigid demand" are more likely to attract buyers; ② **data cannot be reviewed**: figures such as 811.4 billion, 44.50% and 42.16% are not accompanied on the page by sampling method or sample size, so we can only write "a source states"; ③ **a tendency to glamorise new business formats**: the article mentions the ethical controversy over shared pet walking (animal welfare advocates worry that frequent contact with strangers causes **pet stress**), but the overall tone is one of market opportunity.
- **How different people understand it differently**: an entrepreneur reading "44.5% of people keep pets to relieve stress" treats it as **demand validation**; a reader with an animal welfare perspective reading the same sentence asks "**in order to relieve their own stress, are people transferring the cost to the pet?**" — which is precisely the line that "respecting the cat's natural behaviour" in our HMW has to hold.

### C. Social news and in-depth reporting (S3)
- **Who made it**: research by a journalist from *Ban Yue Tan* (a current affairs publication under Xinhua News Agency), reprinted by Beijing Daily.
- **Purpose**: public policy reporting — to set out the social risks of living alone (no one to respond to a sudden illness, gaps in the system for medical consent, emotional exhaustion) and to **offer policy recommendations**.
- **Biases**: ① **problem orientation**: reporting of this kind naturally focuses on risks and extreme cases (people living alone falling unconscious, suicide rate comparisons), which leads readers to systematically **overestimate the danger of living alone**; ② **"keeping pets" is only one item on a list of suggestions here**, alongside "interest buddies" and "mutual-aid communities", and the report provides no empirical data showing that "keeping pets improves the mood of young people living alone" — treating it as evidence of demand is **over-interpretation**; ③ reprinting may cut the original context.
- **How different people understand it differently**: policy researchers read **governance solutions** (grid-based monitoring, designated guardianship); we read **the existence of the group** (one-person households 19.5%, 150–200 million by 2030); while a young person living alone reading the same article may feel **problematised** ("I am described as a risk group that needs to be monitored") — which is a reminder that in interviews we should **not use framings such as "lonely" or "pitiable"** in our questions.

### D. Peer-reviewed research and veterinary guidelines (S4, S5, S6, S7)
- **Who made it**: S4 is by a team at the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, published in PLOS ONE (open access, peer reviewed, explicitly listing the funder Formas and stating that "the funders had no role in study design, analysis or publication"); S7 is by de Mouzon et al. at Paris Nanterre University, published in *Animal Cognition*, and what I read is **Springer Nature's press release** rather than the full paper; S5 is the MSD Veterinary Manual (both author and reviewer are veterinary behaviour specialists, reviewed in July 2025, with references explicitly listed); S6 is FelineVMA's guidelines news release.
- **Purpose**: knowledge production and guidance for clinical practice. **The purpose of these sources is not to sell things**, but each has its own internal incentives — academic publication rewards **novel, significant** results; clinical guidelines reward **actionable, teachable** conclusions.
- **Biases**: ① **publication bias**: the authors of S4 themselves point out that previous research focused on "cats with separation-related problems", while how "most cats that do not show abnormal behaviour respond to being left alone **has not been studied**" — meaning the literature as a whole skews towards **problem cats**; ② **sample bias (the most important point in this document)**: S4 = 14 cats from single-cat households in Sweden, S7 = 16 domestic cats in France, both small samples of indoor cats in Western households; ③ **intermediary bias in press releases**: what I read for S7 is an **institutional press release**, which naturally selects the more "appealing" conclusion ("cats really do care about you"), whereas the effect sizes and statistical details in the paper may be more conservative; ④ **species–scenario mismatch**: these experiments study "brief separation of cat and owner under the same roof", **not "remote interaction through a camera"** — transferring the conclusions to our product scenario is itself an unvalidated inference.
- **How different people understand it differently**: the pet industry reads it as "cats love their owners → there is a market for remote companionship"; behavioural professionals read it as "cats have their own rhythm → the less intervention the better, and it must be predictable"; **we should read it as both at once** — which is exactly why "feel informed and connected" and "respecting the cat's natural behaviour" in the HMW must be satisfied simultaneously.

### E. User reviews and community content (S8, S9, S11, including the S15 snapshot)
- **Who made it**: S8 is a third-party site (JustUseApp) **reprocessing** App Store reviews (and that site itself is supported by traffic referral/advertising, giving the same app opaque scores such as "Safety Score 32.9/100"); S9 is **the vendor's own store listing** (with the rating hosted by Apple); S11 is **AI-generated aggregation content** with an unidentified author and no signed professional background, whose value lies only in its **list of traceable reference sources**.
- **Purpose**: S8/S9 serve downloads and conversion (or, inversely, warning traffic); S11 serves platform content volume and sales conversion (it explicitly discusses the "membership with free hardware" business model).
- **Biases**: ① **extreme-experience bias** (see B2): those who write long reviews are angry paying users and new users who have just unboxed the product, while the silent majority in between goes unrecorded; ② **platform filtering**: the official store can use review-prompt pop-ups and ranking to influence what is shown, while an aggregation site's selection and NLP classification rules are not public; ③ **the risk of "hallucination + rewriting" in AI aggregation**: I **cannot trace the figures and quotations in S11 back, item by item, to the original Xiaohongshu/Zhihu posts** (both sites refused me access), and any one of them may have been distorted in retelling; ④ **promotional content mixed in**: S11 itself admits that the posts it cites include promotional articles.
- **How different people understand it differently**: a product manager reading "the voice scares the cat" wants to **optimise the audio quality**; a behavioural perspective reads it as "**perhaps it should not make a sound unprompted at all**"; while a cat owner working late who reads "the sensible use of monitoring is to reduce the number of times you open it, not to increase it" (S11) will react quite differently — some feel understood, some feel accused. This quotation is **a strong pointer** for our product positioning ("reassurance" rather than "addiction"), but it is **second-hand citation**, and if it is to enter the deck its source level should be labelled.

### F. Vendor official pages (S12, S13)
- **Who made it**: the respective marketing teams of Furbo (Tomofun) and Petcube.
- **Purpose**: **direct selling** — every feature description on the page points to "making you place an order" (S12's first screen is "first device free + 30 days of Nanny" and "$10 off"; S13's first screen is the discounted price and a "45-day money-back guarantee").
- **Biases**: ① **selective presentation**: Petcube claims that its two-way audio delivers "natural, lifelike sound so your pet recognises your voice" (S13) — this claim is **partly supported** by the experiment in S7, but user feedback in S8/S15 shows that in practice the pipeline often cannot achieve it, and the official website will not mention this gap; ② **ratings presented by the vendor**: the 4.3/5 in S13 and the 4.7/5 in S9 are both ratings that have been put on display, with negative long reviews kept off the first screen; ③ **hidden costs in the price structure**: key information such as subscription renewal prices, the surcharge for additional cameras and the fact that "the no-subscription version has no AI alerts" (S15) is scattered across different pages.
- **How different people understand it differently**: an investor reads **a validated subscription business model**; a consumer-rights perspective reads "**you buy the hardware and still have to pay every month**"; we read "**the industry puts AI capabilities behind a paywall** — so who exactly should pay for 'understanding cats', and how much" — a product question that has not yet been answered.

### G. Commercial media reviews and AI-generated reviews (S14, S10)
- **Who made it**: S14 is by a consumer technology journalist at The Irish Times (by-lined Ciara O'Brien, with an expectation of editorial process and fact-checking); S10 is a Sohu self-media account whose page **carries its own statement that it "contains AI-generated content"**, with no by-line and no verifiable professional background for the author.
- **Purpose**: S14 serves readers' decisions (and its body text is behind a paywall, with subscription as the business model); S10 serves **traffic and sales** (tying in a promotion at the end and prompting interaction in the comments).
- **Biases**: S14 may have a mild tendency arising from **a loan review unit/public relations relationship**, but its subtitle frankly gives a negative judgement ("far from essential", "the cat was less impressed"), making it **a restrained, citable third-party opinion**; S10, by contrast, is a sample **systematically biased towards a single brand**, and all the third-party data sources it cites (LabPet, iFlytek AI Lab, the iResearch white paper) are ones I **cannot verify**, amounting to **unverifiable appeal to authority**.
- **How different people understand it differently**: an ordinary consumer reading S10 concludes that "Furbo is simply the best solution"; someone trained in media studies first asks "**who is LabPet? Where is this report? Why does a review have to tie in a 618 promotion?**". The difference between these two kinds of reader is exactly the concrete example we can offer when demonstrating "media literacy" in the deck.
